Module 1

Excel Basics & Navigation

Welcome to Excel – your new superpower.

What you will achieve by the end of this module:
  • Confidently open Excel and identify every major part of the screen.
  • Understand rows, columns, cells, and ranges like the back of your hand.
  • Enter, edit, copy, and paste data without hesitation.
  • Know the difference between a workbook and a worksheet.
  • Navigate large spreadsheets like a pro using keyboard shortcuts.
  • Save, open, and manage your Excel files effectively.
Think of Excel as a giant, smart grid. We'll start with the absolute basics – no prior experience needed. Every expert was once a beginner!

What is Excel? (With Real-World Examples)

Excel is a spreadsheet program that helps you store, calculate, and analyze data. It's used by over 750 million people worldwide across every industry.

Personal Budget
Track income & expenses. Excel sums everything and shows your savings. Create charts to visualize spending patterns.
Shop Inventory
List products, quantities, and prices. Excel calculates total stock value and can alert you when items are low.
Student Grades
Enter scores, calculate averages, assign Pass/Fail automatically. Track class performance with charts.
Sales Analysis
Track sales by region, product, and salesperson. Find trends, best performers, and growth opportunities.
HR Management
Track employee data, attendance, salaries, and performance reviews all in one place.
Project Planning
Create Gantt charts, track milestones, manage budgets, and monitor progress.
Excel is used in almost every industry – from finance to healthcare, education to engineering. It's a skill that pays off!

Your Excel Screen – A Detailed Tour

  • Ribbon: The toolbar at the top with tabs (Home, Insert, Data, Review, View). Everything you need is organized here in logical groups.
  • Quick Access Toolbar: Small toolbar above the ribbon – add your most-used commands (Save, Undo, Redo, Print) for one-click access.
  • Formula Bar: Shows the content of the selected cell – great for editing long formulas and seeing exactly what's in a cell.
  • Name Box: Displays the cell address (e.g., A1). You can also name cells here for easier reference in formulas.
  • Rows: Numbered 1, 2, 3… (horizontal). There are over 1 million rows in Excel 2016+.
  • Columns: Lettered A, B, C… (vertical). There are over 16,000 columns (A to XFD).
  • Cells: The intersection of a row and column (e.g., B5). Each cell can hold text, numbers, or formulas.
  • Sheet Tabs: At the bottom – you can have multiple sheets in one workbook. Right-click for options like rename, move, copy, or color.
  • Status Bar: Shows useful info like sum, average, and count of selected cells. Right-click to customize what appears.
  • Scroll Bars: Vertical and horizontal scroll bars for navigating large sheets.
  • Zoom Slider: At the bottom right – zoom in/out for better visibility.
Analogy: A workbook is a whole notebook. Each worksheet is one page. Cells are the tiny boxes where you write. The ribbon is like a toolbox with all your tools organized.

Rows, Columns, Cells & Ranges Explained

Row – horizontal, numbered (1, 2, 3…). Each row contains data for a single record.

Column – vertical, lettered (A, B, C…). Each column contains a specific type of data (e.g., Name, Date, Amount).

Cell – a single box: A1 means column A, row 1. The active cell is highlighted with a border.

Range – a group of cells, e.g., A1:C5 includes columns A to C and rows 1 to 5. Ranges are used in formulas and functions.

Example: If you want to add numbers from A1 to A5, you use the range A1:A5 in a SUM function.

CellColumnRowExample Use
A1A1Starting point, usually a header
B5B5Specific data point in column B
D10D10Formula reference location
G25G25Data entry cell
Always remember: column comes first, row second – just like coordinates on a map! (A1 = Column A, Row 1)

Entering & Editing Data – Step by Step

  • Enter: Click a cell, type your data, press Enter to move down, or Tab to move right.
  • Edit: Double-click the cell, or press F2, or just type over it. Use the Formula Bar for longer edits.
  • Copy: Ctrl+C – Cut: Ctrl+X – Paste: Ctrl+V – works just like in any other program.
  • Undo: Ctrl+Z – your best friend when learning! You can undo multiple steps.
  • Redo: Ctrl+Y – if you undo too far, you can redo.
  • Delete: Delete key clears content, Backspace removes characters one by one.
  • Select All: Ctrl+A – selects the entire data range or the whole sheet.
  • Paste Special: Alt+E, S or right-click → Paste Special – paste values, formats, or formulas only.

Practice: Type your name in A1, your age in B1, your city in C1. Press Enter after each. Now click A1, press F2, change your name, press Enter. See how easy that is?

Keyboard shortcut tip: Ctrl+Arrow jumps to the edge of your data – great for navigating large spreadsheets!

Data Types in Excel – What You Can Store

  • Text (Label): "Emmanuel", "Lagos", "Product A" – left-aligned by default. Also called strings.
  • Numbers: 100, 25.5, -10, 0.05, 1,234,567 – right-aligned by default. Can be used in calculations.
  • Dates: 01/01/2026, 15-Jul-2026, 2026-07-15 – Excel stores dates as serial numbers (1 = Jan 1, 1900).
  • Time: 8:00 AM, 14:30, 22:15 – also stored as decimal numbers (0.5 = 12:00 PM).
  • Formulas: =A1+B1 – always starts with = and calculates a result.
  • Logical (Boolean): TRUE or FALSE – used in decision-making and conditional formatting.
  • Errors: #DIV/0!, #N/A, #VALUE!, #REF!, #NAME? – Excel tells you when something's wrong with helpful error codes.
  • Hyperlinks: Clickable links to websites, files, or email addresses.
Excel treats text and numbers differently. Numbers can be used in calculations; text cannot. Dates are actually numbers behind the scenes – that's why you can subtract them to get days! This is called "date serial numbers."

Workbook vs Worksheet – What's the Difference?

  • Workbook: The entire Excel file (like a notebook). It can contain many sheets. File extension: .xlsx (standard), .xlsm (with macros).
  • Worksheet: One page/tab inside the workbook (like a page in a notebook). Also called a sheet.
  • How to add a new worksheet: Click the + icon at the bottom, or press Shift+F11.
  • How to rename: Double-click the sheet tab and type a new name. Use descriptive names like "Data", "Analysis", "Dashboard".
  • How to delete: Right-click the sheet tab → Delete. Be careful – this cannot be undone!
  • How to move: Drag the sheet tab to a new position. You can also move sheets between workbooks.
  • How to color: Right-click → Tab Color – helps organize visually with color coding.
  • How to copy: Right-click → Move or Copy → Check "Create a copy".
  • How to hide: Right-click → Hide – useful for hiding reference sheets.
Best practice: Name your sheets clearly (e.g., "Raw Data", "Calculations", "Charts", "Dashboard") so you can navigate easily. Use a consistent naming convention.

Navigating Excel Like a Pro

  • Ctrl + Home – Jump to cell A1 instantly.
  • Ctrl + End – Jump to the last cell with data in the sheet.
  • Ctrl + Arrow – Jump to the edge of your data region (great for large datasets).
  • Ctrl + Shift + Arrow – Select all cells from current to the edge – quickly highlight large ranges.
  • Ctrl + Page Up / Page Down – Switch between sheets quickly.
  • F5 – Go to any cell by typing its address – opens the Go To dialog.
  • Ctrl + Space – Select the entire current column.
  • Shift + Space – Select the entire current row.
  • Ctrl + Shift + L – Toggle filters on/off.
  • Ctrl + [ – Go to the cell that a formula references (trace precedents).
  • Ctrl + ] – Go to cells that depend on the current cell (trace dependents).
These shortcuts will save you hours of scrolling and clicking. Practice them from day one – they'll become second nature!

Saving Your Work – Essential Skills

  • Save: Ctrl+S – save your work frequently! Excel doesn't auto-save by default (unless you use OneDrive).
  • Save As: F12 – save with a new name or location. Great for creating versions.
  • File types: .xlsx (standard workbook), .xls (older format), .csv (comma-separated values for data exchange).
  • AutoRecover: Excel automatically saves versions – File → Info → Manage Workbook → Recover Unsaved Workbooks.
  • Backup tip: Save a copy before making major changes (e.g., "Budget_v1.xlsx", "Budget_v2.xlsx").
  • Export: File → Export – save as PDF for sharing without allowing edits.
  • Cloud saves: Save to OneDrive or SharePoint for automatic backup and collaboration.
Golden rule: Save early, save often! Ctrl+S should be muscle memory. Every 5 minutes is a good habit.

Opening and Managing Files

  • Open: Ctrl+O – browse to open an existing file.
  • Recent files: File → Open → Recent – quickly access files you've worked on recently.
  • New workbook: Ctrl+N – create a new blank workbook.
  • Templates: File → New – use pre-designed templates for budgets, calendars, invoices, etc.
  • Pin files: Pin frequently used files to the top of the Recent list for quick access.
  • File properties: File → Info – view and edit file properties (author, title, comments).
Organization tip: Create a dedicated folder for your Excel files. Use subfolders for different projects or categories.

Excel Views – See Your Data Differently

  • Normal view: The default view – good for data entry and editing.
  • Page Layout view: See how your sheet will look when printed – shows margins, headers, and footers.
  • Page Break Preview: See where Excel will split pages when printing – adjust breaks manually.
  • Custom Views: Save specific zoom levels, hidden rows/columns, and print settings – great for reports.
  • Full Screen: View → Full Screen (or Alt+V, U) – maximize your workspace.
  • Split view: View → Split – divide your sheet into panes that scroll independently.
Use Page Layout view when you're preparing a report – it shows exactly what will print.

Module 2

Formatting & Basic Formulas

Make your data look professional and start doing math.

By the end of this module you will:
  • Format fonts, colors, borders, and number formats like currency and percentage.
  • Adjust column width and wrap text for readability.
  • Write formulas using addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division.
  • Use SUM and AVERAGE to calculate totals and averages.
  • Use MIN, MAX, COUNT, and COUNTA to summarize data.
  • Understand cell references and how to copy formulas.

Formatting – Make Your Data Look Professional

  • Font & Size: Use bold for headings, larger text for titles. Arial, Calibri, or Segoe UI are great choices for readability.
  • Colors: Use cell fill to separate sections (e.g., header row in dark blue with white text). Be consistent with your color scheme.
  • Borders: Add gridlines to make tables easy to read. Use "All Borders" for a clean look, or "Thick Outside Borders" for emphasis.
  • Number Format: Change General to Currency ($), Percentage (%), Date, or Accounting. Right-click → Format Cells (or Ctrl+1).
  • Column Width: Double-click the line between column letters to AutoFit – instant perfect width. Or drag to adjust manually.
  • Wrap Text: Long text appears on multiple lines inside the cell – no more cut-off text. Great for descriptions.
  • Merge & Center: Combine cells for titles – but avoid merging inside your data table (it causes issues with sorting and filtering).
  • Format Painter: Copy formatting from one cell to another – Ctrl+Shift+C and Ctrl+Shift+V or use the paintbrush icon.
  • Cell Styles: Use predefined styles for headings, totals, and accents – consistent and fast.
A well-formatted sheet is easier to understand, looks more professional, and reduces errors. Good formatting is good communication.

Number Formatting – Make Numbers Meaningful

  • General: Default format – no specific formatting.
  • Number: Controls decimal places and thousands separators (e.g., 1,234.56).
  • Currency: Adds currency symbol ($, €, £) and decimal places – perfect for financial data.
  • Accounting: Similar to Currency but aligns currency symbols and decimal points.
  • Percentage: Multiplies by 100 and adds % sign – essential for rates and ratios.
  • Date: Displays dates in various formats (e.g., 01/01/2026, Jan 1, 2026).
  • Time: Displays time formats (e.g., 8:00 AM, 14:30).
  • Fraction: Displays numbers as fractions (e.g., 1/2, 3/4).
  • Scientific: Displays numbers in scientific notation (e.g., 1.23E+05).
  • Text: Treats numbers as text – prevents Excel from auto-formatting (e.g., leading zeros in product codes).
Tip: Use the Format Cells dialog (Ctrl+1) for full control over number formatting – you can create custom formats too!

Formulas – The Heart of Excel

Every formula starts with =. Example: =10+5 → 15.

But instead of typing numbers, use cell references – that way Excel updates automatically when you change values. This is called "dynamic calculation."

=A2 * B2 (if A2=10, B2=5 → result is 50)

Basic Operators:

  • + Addition – adds numbers together
  • - Subtraction – subtracts numbers
  • * Multiplication – multiplies numbers
  • / Division – divides numbers (cannot divide by zero!)
  • ^ Power (e.g., =A1^2 for square, =A1^3 for cube)
  • % Percentage (e.g., =A1*10% for 10% of A1)

Remember the order of operations (PEMDAS: Parentheses, Exponents, Multiplication/Division, Addition/Subtraction). Use brackets when needed: =(10+5)*2 → 30.

You can see all formulas in a sheet by pressing Ctrl+` (grave accent, usually next to the 1 key).

SUM – Add Numbers Instantly

=SUM(A1:A5) – adds all numbers from A1 to A5. This is much faster than typing =A1+A2+A3+A4+A5.

Example: You have sales figures in column C from row 2 to row 20.

=SUM(C2:C20) → total sales

You can also add individual cells: =SUM(A1, B1, C1) – great for non-contiguous cells.

You can add entire columns: =SUM(C:C) – adds everything in column C.

Keyboard shortcut: Alt + = automatically adds SUM to your selected range – instant total!

SUM is the most used function in Excel. Master it and you'll save hours of manual addition. It's the foundation of data analysis.

AVERAGE – Find the Middle Value

=AVERAGE(B2:B10) – calculates the average of the range. Useful for understanding typical values.

Example: You have test scores from 10 students. The average tells you the overall performance of the class.

=AVERAGE(D2:D11) → average score

If you have 70, 80, 90 – the average is 80.

Real-world use: Calculate average monthly expenses, average sales per day, average customer satisfaction score, average delivery time.

AVERAGE is great for finding typical values – but watch out for outliers (extremely high or low values) that can skew the result. Consider using MEDIAN for skewed data.

MIN, MAX, COUNT, COUNTA – Quick Summaries

  • =MIN(D2:D50) – smallest value in the range. Great for finding the lowest price, minimum score, or earliest date.
  • =MAX(D2:D50) – largest value. Find the highest salary, best sales day, top score, or latest date.
  • =COUNT(E2:E50) – counts only cells with numbers. Useful for counting numerical entries like sales figures.
  • =COUNTA(E2:E50) – counts all non-empty cells (text, numbers, dates). Counts everything that's filled in.
  • =COUNTBLANK(E2:E50) – counts empty cells. Helpful for finding missing data.
  • =COUNTIF(E2:E50, ">100") – counts cells that meet a condition (e.g., sales over 100).

Example: In a salary list, MIN gives the lowest salary, MAX gives the highest, COUNT tells you how many salaries are entered, and COUNTA tells you how many employees have any data.

These functions help you quickly understand your data without manually searching through hundreds of rows – instant insights!

Quick Analysis Tool – Excel's Hidden Gem

Select a range of data and look for the small Quick Analysis icon that appears at the bottom-right corner. This tool is perfect for beginners!

  • Formatting: Apply conditional formatting, data bars, color scales, and icon sets instantly.
  • Charts: Instantly create column, line, pie, and bar charts from your data.
  • Totals: Quickly add SUM, AVERAGE, COUNT, or other functions below or to the right of your data.
  • Tables: Convert your data into a formatted table with one click.
  • Sparklines: Add mini charts inside cells to show trends at a glance.
This tool is perfect for beginners – it does the heavy lifting for you and shows you what's possible. Use it to explore different ways to present your data!

Module 3

Functions, IF & Text Functions

Make Excel think, decide, and clean your text.

By the end of this module you will:
  • Use the IF function to make decisions (Pass/Fail, Target Met/Not Met).
  • Combine conditions with AND and OR for complex logic.
  • Clean and transform text using UPPER, LOWER, PROPER, TRIM, LEFT, RIGHT, and MID.
  • Understand relative vs absolute references and when to use each.
  • Use CONCAT and TEXTJOIN to combine text from multiple cells.
  • Use FIND and SEARCH to locate text within cells.

IF Function – Excel Makes Decisions

The IF function is one of Excel's most powerful tools. It checks a condition and returns one value if true, another if false. This is called "conditional logic."

=IF(condition, value_if_true, value_if_false)

Example 1 – Pass/Fail: A student scores 60. Pass mark is 50.

=IF(A2>=50, "Pass", "Fail")

If A2=60 → "Pass". If A2=40 → "Fail".

Example 2 – Sales Target: If sales are at least $1,000, "Target Met"; otherwise "Target Not Met".

=IF(B2>=1000, "Target Met", "Target Not Met")

Example 3 – Discount: If order value is over $100, give 10% discount.

=IF(A2>100, A2*0.9, A2)
You can use IF with numbers, text, dates, and even other functions. The possibilities are endless!

Nested IF – Multiple Conditions

Sometimes you need more than two outcomes. You can nest IF functions inside each other – this is called a "nested IF."

=IF(A2>=90, "A", IF(A2>=80, "B", IF(A2>=70, "C", "Fail")))

Example – Grade System:

  • 90+ → "A" (Excellent)
  • 80-89 → "B" (Good)
  • 70-79 → "C" (Average)
  • Below 70 → "Fail" (Needs Improvement)

Example 2 – Commission:

=IF(Sales>=10000, Sales*0.15, IF(Sales>=5000, Sales*0.10, IF(Sales>=1000, Sales*0.05, 0)))
Nested IFs are powerful but can become complex. For more than 3 conditions, consider using IFS (Excel 2019+) or a lookup table.

AND & OR – Multiple Conditions Together

AND – all conditions must be true. Returns TRUE only if every condition is satisfied.

=AND(A2>=50, B2>=50) → true only if both scores are 50+

OR – at least one condition is true. Returns TRUE if any condition is satisfied.

=OR(A2>=50, B2>=50) → true if either score is 50+

Combined with IF:

=IF(AND(A2>=50, B2>=50), "Eligible", "Not Eligible")

Real-world example 1: =IF(AND(Sales>=1000, CustomerRating>=4), "Bonus", "No Bonus")

Real-world example 2: =IF(OR(Department="Sales", Department="Marketing"), "Team A", "Team B")

AND and OR help you build complex business logic without complicated formulas. They're essential for data analysis.

Text Functions – Clean & Transform Your Data

  • =UPPER(A2) → "emmanuel" becomes "EMMANUEL" – great for consistency in codes and IDs.
  • =LOWER(A2) → "EMMANUEL" becomes "emmanuel" – useful for email addresses.
  • =PROPER(A2) → "emmanuel iheomamere" becomes "Emmanuel Iheomamere" – perfect for names and titles.
  • =TRIM(A2) – removes extra spaces. Essential for cleaning imported data from other systems.
  • =LEFT(A2, n) – returns the first n characters (e.g., =LEFT("Hello", 2) → "He").
  • =RIGHT(A2, n) – returns the last n characters (e.g., =RIGHT("Hello", 2) → "lo").
  • =MID(A2, start, n) – returns n characters starting from a position (e.g., =MID("Hello", 2, 3) → "ell").
  • =LEN(A2) – counts the number of characters in a cell (e.g., =LEN("Hello") → 5).
  • =FIND(" ", A2) – finds the position of a space (or any character) – great for splitting text.
  • =SEARCH(" ", A2) – similar to FIND but not case-sensitive.
TRIM is a lifesaver – it removes hidden spaces that can break your formulas and lookups. Always use TRIM on imported data!

Relative vs Absolute References – The Key to Copying Formulas

Relative: =A2*B2 – when you copy this formula down, it changes to =A3*B3, =A4*B4… This is the default behavior and is useful for row-by-row calculations.

Absolute: =$B$1 – the dollar signs lock both the column and row. No matter where you copy it, it always refers to B1. Essential for constants like tax rates.

=A2 * $B$1 (B1 is fixed, e.g., tax rate 10%)

Mixed references: $A1 (column locked, row relative) – use when you want to copy across rows but keep the same column. A$1 (row locked, column relative) – use when you want to copy across columns but keep the same row.

Tip: Press F4 while editing a cell reference to cycle through relative, absolute, and mixed references – a huge time-saver!

CONCAT & TEXTJOIN – Combine Text from Multiple Cells

=CONCAT(A2, " ", B2) – combines A2 and B2 with a space in between. Simple and effective.

Example: A2 = "Emmanuel", B2 = "Iheomamere" → "Emmanuel Iheomamere"

=CONCAT(A2, " ", B2) → "Emmanuel Iheomamere"

TEXTJOIN – more powerful, with a delimiter and option to ignore empty cells. Perfect for lists.

=TEXTJOIN(", ", TRUE, A2:A10) → "John, Mary, Peter, ..."

Real-world uses:

  • Combine first and last names into full names.
  • Create full addresses from street, city, state, and zip.
  • Build unique IDs (e.g., =CONCAT("EMP-", A2, "-", B2)).
  • Create comma-separated lists for email campaigns or reports.
You can also use the ampersand: =A2 & " " & B2 – faster and simpler for small combinations!

FIND & SEARCH – Locate Text Within Text

These functions find the position of a substring within a cell – useful for extracting parts of text.

  • =FIND(" ", A2) – finds the position of the first space in A2. Returns a number (e.g., 8).
  • =SEARCH(" ", A2) – same as FIND but not case-sensitive.
  • Example: Extract first name from "Emmanuel Iheomamere" – =LEFT(A2, FIND(" ", A2)-1) → "Emmanuel".
  • Example: Extract last name – =RIGHT(A2, LEN(A2)-FIND(" ", A2)) → "Iheomamere".
  • Example: Extract domain from email – =RIGHT(A2, LEN(A2)-FIND("@", A2)) → "gmail.com".
Combine FIND/SEARCH with LEFT, RIGHT, and MID to extract specific parts of text – essential for data cleaning.

Module 4

Data Cleaning, Sorting, Filtering & Tables

Turn messy data into a structured, usable format.

By the end of this module you will:
  • Sort data alphabetically or numerically in ascending or descending order.
  • Filter data to show only what you need.
  • Create Excel Tables (Ctrl+T) for dynamic, organized data.
  • Remove duplicates, use Find & Replace, and split text with Text to Columns.
  • Use Data Validation to create dropdown lists and prevent errors.
  • Clean and prepare data for analysis.

Sort – Organize Your Data

Sort arranges data in order – A→Z, Z→A, smallest to largest, oldest to newest. This makes data easier to analyze.

How to sort:

  • Select your data range (including headers).
  • Go to Data → Sort.
  • Choose the column to sort by and the order (Ascending or Descending).
  • You can also sort by multiple columns (e.g., first by Department, then by Salary) – great for hierarchical sorting.
  • For quick sorting, use the Sort buttons on the Data tab – A→Z (ascending) or Z→A (descending).
  • Custom sort: Sort by cell color, font color, or icon – useful for visually categorized data.
Pro tip: Always select your entire data range before sorting – otherwise, only one column will be sorted and your data will be misaligned!

Filter – Show Only What You Need

Filter shows only the rows that meet a condition. For example, show only customers from "Enugu" or sales above $1,000.

  • Select your data range.
  • Go to Data → Filter (or press Ctrl+Shift+L).
  • Click the dropdown arrow in the column header.
  • Select the value you want to filter by (e.g., "Enugu").
  • You can also filter by color, text, numbers, or dates with advanced filter options.
  • Use text filters like "Contains" or "Begins With" for partial matches – great for searching.
  • Use number filters like "Greater Than" or "Between" for numeric ranges.
  • Use date filters like "This Month" or "Last Year" for time-based data.
Filtering does not delete data – it just hides other rows temporarily. Clear the filter to see everything again. The row numbers turn blue when a filter is active.

Excel Tables – Your New Best Friend (Ctrl+T)

Convert your data range into a table with Ctrl+T. Tables are one of Excel's most powerful features for managing data.

  • Automatic filters – every table comes with built-in filters on each column header.
  • Alternating row colors – easier to read and more professional.
  • Formulas auto‑extend – when you add a new row, formulas copy down automatically – no more dragging!
  • Structured references – use column names instead of cell references (e.g., =SUM(Table1[Sales])) – much clearer.
  • Auto-expand – when you type in the row below, the table automatically includes it.
  • Better for PivotTables and charts – tables update automatically when you add data.
  • Total row – add a total row with SUM, AVERAGE, COUNT, and more.
  • Table styles – choose from many predefined styles for a polished look.

Golden rules for good data:

  • One row = one record (one customer, one product, one transaction).
  • One column = one type of information (Name, Date, Amount, etc.).
  • No blank rows inside the table.
  • No merged cells inside the data area.
  • Use clear, descriptive headers in the first row.

Remove Duplicates – Clean Your Data

Duplicate records can cause errors in analysis, skew results, and waste storage space. Remove them easily:

  • Select your data range or table.
  • Go to Data → Remove Duplicates.
  • Choose which columns to check for duplicates (select all columns for exact duplicates).
  • Click OK – Excel removes duplicate rows and tells you how many were removed.
  • You can also remove duplicates based on multiple columns (e.g., duplicate name AND address – keep the first occurrence).
  • For tables, you can use the "Remove Duplicates" button in the Table Design tab.
Always keep a backup before removing duplicates – you can't undo this action! Save a copy first or copy the data to another sheet.

Find & Replace (Ctrl+H) – Make Bulk Changes

Change values across your entire workbook in seconds. This is much faster than manually editing each cell.

  • Press Ctrl+H to open Find & Replace.
  • In "Find what:", type the value you want to change (e.g., "Enugu State").
  • In "Replace with:", type the new value (e.g., "Enugu").
  • Click "Replace All" – Excel changes every instance in the selected range or entire sheet.
  • You can also use Find & Replace to change formatting, not just values (click Options → Format).
  • Use "Options" to match case (uppercase/lowercase) or match entire cell contents.
  • You can search within formulas, values, or comments.
  • Use "Find" (Ctrl+F) to locate specific values without changing them.
Find & Replace is incredibly powerful – you can change hundreds of entries in seconds. Use it to clean up inconsistent data!

Data Validation – Dropdown Lists & Error Prevention

Control what users can enter. For example, instead of typing a state, they choose from a dropdown list – this prevents typos and inconsistencies.

  • Select the cells where you want the dropdown.
  • Go to Data → Data Validation.
  • Under "Allow:", select "List".
  • In "Source:", type your options separated by commas (e.g., "Enugu, Lagos, Abuja, Kano").
  • You can also use a range of cells as the source (e.g., =$A$1:$A$10) – great for dynamic lists.
  • Add an "Input Message" to guide users on what to enter.
  • Add an "Error Alert" to prevent invalid entries – choose Stop, Warning, or Information.
  • Other validation types: Whole Number, Decimal, Date, Time, Text Length, and Custom formula.
  • You can also create a dropdown that shows values from a named range.
This is one of the best ways to ensure data quality in shared workbooks. No typos, no inconsistencies, and users appreciate the guidance.

Text to Columns – Split Data Into Multiple Columns

Example: You have a column with full names: "Emmanuel Iheomamere". You want first name and last name in separate columns for sorting or analysis.

  • Select the column you want to split.
  • Go to Data → Text to Columns.
  • Choose "Delimited" (if your data has a separator like space, comma, or tab).
  • Select the delimiter (e.g., "Space").
  • Choose the destination (where to put the new columns).
  • Click Finish – now you have two columns!
  • You can also use "Fixed Width" if your data is aligned in columns (like fixed-format reports).

This is also great for splitting addresses (street, city, state, zip), product codes, dates, or any data with a consistent separator.

You can also use Flash Fill (Ctrl+E) for quick splitting without formulas – just type the first result and Excel fills the rest!

Lookups, Dates, Conditional Formatting

VLOOKUP – Find Information Fast

VLOOKUP (Vertical Lookup) searches for a value in the first column of a table and returns something from another column. It's one of the most used functions in Excel.

=VLOOKUP(lookup_value, table_array, col_index_num, [range_lookup])

Example: You have a table with Employee ID, Name, and Department. You type an ID in E2, and VLOOKUP returns the Name.

IDNameDepartment
001JohnFinance
002MaryHR
003PeterIT
=VLOOKUP("002", A2:C4, 2, FALSE) → "Mary"
Important: VLOOKUP always searches in the first column. The value you're looking for must be in the leftmost column. Use FALSE for exact matches (always use FALSE unless you have a good reason).

XLOOKUP – The Modern Replacement

XLOOKUP is more flexible than VLOOKUP (available in Excel 365 and Excel 2021). It's the future of lookups in Excel.

=XLOOKUP(lookup_value, lookup_array, return_array, [if_not_found])

Example: Search for ID in A2:A4, return the Name from B2:B4. If not found, return "Not Found".

=XLOOKUP(E2, A2:A4, B2:B4, "Not Found")
Advantages over VLOOKUP:
  • Can search in any column, not just the first.
  • Can return values from any column (no need to count columns).
  • Can search from bottom to top (with search_mode).
  • Works with both vertical and horizontal data.
  • Has a built-in "if_not_found" parameter – no more #N/A errors!
  • More intuitive and easier to learn.

INDEX & MATCH – The Flexible Alternative

INDEX & MATCH is a powerful combination that offers more flexibility than VLOOKUP. It's used by advanced Excel users.

  • =MATCH(lookup_value, lookup_array, [match_type]) – finds the position of a value in a range.
  • =INDEX(return_array, row_num, [column_num]) – returns a value from a range at a specific position.
  • Combined: =INDEX(B2:B4, MATCH(E2, A2:A4, 0)) – finds the name matching the ID in E2.
=INDEX(B2:B4, MATCH(E2, A2:A4, 0))

Advantages:

  • Can search in any column, not just the first.
  • Can search from right to left (unlike VLOOKUP).
  • More flexible with dynamic ranges.
  • Faster on large datasets.
INDEX & MATCH is worth learning – it gives you complete control over lookups and works in all Excel versions.

Working with Dates – Extract Day, Month, Year

  • =DAY(A2) – extract day (e.g., 15) from a date.
  • =MONTH(A2) – extract month (e.g., 7 for July).
  • =YEAR(A2) – extract year (e.g., 2026).
  • =DATE(year, month, day) – create a date from numbers.
  • =DATEDIF(A2, B2, "Y") – complete years between two dates.
  • =DATEDIF(A2, B2, "M") – complete months between two dates.
  • =DATEDIF(A2, B2, "D") – total days between two dates.
  • =TODAY() – today's date (updates automatically).
  • =NOW() – today's date and time.
  • =EDATE(A2, 3) – add 3 months to a date (e.g., for expiry dates).
  • =EOMONTH(A2, 0) – last day of the month (great for financial reporting).
  • =WORKDAY(A2, 5) – add 5 working days (excludes weekends).
  • =NETWORKDAYS(A2, B2) – number of working days between two dates.
Practical: Calculate someone's age: =DATEDIF(BirthDate, TODAY(), "Y") – works perfectly!

Conditional Formatting – Visual Intelligence

Cells change color automatically based on rules. This helps you spot patterns, outliers, and trends instantly.

  • Highlight Cell Rules: Greater than, Less than, Between, Equal to, Duplicate values – great for flagging specific values.
  • Top/Bottom Rules: Top 10%, Bottom 10, Above average, Below average – identify best and worst performers.
  • Color Scales: Gradient colors from low to high – visualize distribution.
  • Data Bars: Bars inside cells that represent values – like a bar chart inside cells.
  • Icon Sets: Use arrows, flags, or traffic lights to show status – perfect for dashboards.
  • Custom formulas: Create your own rules using Excel formulas – unlimited possibilities.
  • Rule management: Manage Rules to see all rules, edit, delete, or change order.
Real-world example: Highlight sales above $1,000 in green, below $500 in red – you can see who's performing well instantly. Great for management reports.

COUNTIFS & SUMIFS – Multiple Conditions

COUNTIFS counts rows that meet several conditions – powerful for multi-dimensional analysis.

=COUNTIFS(A:A, "Enugu", B:B, "Active")

Counts how many rows have "Enugu" in column A AND "Active" in column B.

SUMIFS sums values based on multiple conditions – essential for financial analysis.

=SUMIFS(C:C, A:A, "Enugu", B:B, "Active")

Sums column C where column A = "Enugu" AND column B = "Active".

Examples with more conditions:

  • =SUMIFS(Sales, Region, "North", Product, "Laptop", Year, 2026)
  • =COUNTIFS(Department, "Sales", Performance, ">80") – count high-performing sales employees.
  • =SUMIFS(Revenue, Category, "Electronics", Month, "January")
Use case: Count active patients from Enugu, or sum sales for a specific product in a specific region. These functions are essential for data analysis.

Charts, PivotTables & Final Project

Turn data into stories and build your first dashboard.

By the end of this module you will:
  • Create column, bar, line, and pie charts to visualize data.
  • Use PivotTables to summarize thousands of rows in seconds.
  • Build a mini dashboard with KPIs, charts, and a summary table.
  • Complete a final project that combines everything you've learned.
  • Understand chart types and when to use each.
  • Create interactive dashboards with slicers.

Charts – Visualize Your Data

  • Column Chart: Compare categories (e.g., sales by product). Best for showing differences between items. Use when you have 5-10 categories.
  • Bar Chart: Same as column, but horizontal – good for long category names or many categories.
  • Line Chart: Show trends over time (e.g., monthly sales). Best for continuous data and time series.
  • Pie Chart: Show parts of a whole (e.g., expense breakdown). Best for showing percentages and proportions.
  • Scatter Chart: Show relationships between two variables (e.g., marketing spend vs sales). Use for correlation analysis.
  • Area Chart: Like a line chart but filled – good for showing volume and cumulative totals.
  • Combo Chart: Combine two chart types (e.g., column + line) for different data series – great for comparing different metrics.
  • Histogram: Show frequency distribution – useful for statistical analysis.
  • Waterfall Chart: Show cumulative effect of positive and negative values – perfect for financial statements.
Quick chart: Select your data and press Alt+F1 to create a chart instantly. Use F11 to create a chart on a new sheet.

Chart Design – Make Your Charts Professional

  • Chart Title: Add a clear, descriptive title that tells the story.
  • Axis Labels: Always label your axes – what are you measuring?
  • Data Labels: Show values on bars or data points for clarity.
  • Legend: Use a legend to explain colors or patterns.
  • Colors: Use a consistent color scheme – avoid too many colors.
  • Gridlines: Use minimal gridlines – too many are distracting.
  • Chart Styles: Use the Chart Design tab to apply professional styles and colors.
  • Chart Elements: Add or remove elements like gridlines, data tables, and error bars.
  • Size and Position: Resize and move charts to fit your layout.
Good chart design makes your data easy to understand. A well-designed chart is worth a thousand words!

PivotTables – Summarize Instantly

A PivotTable summarizes large datasets instantly. Imagine you have 10,000 sales records – you can get total sales by region, by product, by month in seconds.

  • Rows: What you want to group by (e.g., Region, Product, Month). Drag fields here to create row labels.
  • Columns: What you want to compare across (e.g., Year, Quarter). Drag fields here to create column labels.
  • Values: What you want to calculate (e.g., Sum of Sales, Average of Sales, Count of Orders). Drag numeric fields here.
  • Filters: Narrow down (e.g., only 2026, only a specific product). Drag fields here to filter the entire table.
  • Value Field Settings: Change from Sum to Average, Count, Min, Max, or % of total – right-click on the value field.
  • Show Values As: Show as % of total, % of row, running total, or rank.
  • Grouping: Group dates by month, quarter, or year. Group numbers into ranges.
  • Refresh: Right-click → Refresh to update when your source data changes.
How to create: Select your data → Insert → PivotTable → Choose where to place it → Drag and drop fields. It's that simple!

PivotTable – Step by Step Example

Example: You have sales data with Region, Product, and Sales Amount.

RegionProductSales
NorthLaptop5000
SouthLaptop7000
NorthPhone3000
SouthPhone4000
EastLaptop6000
WestTablet2000

PivotTable can show:

  • Total sales by Region (North = 8000, South = 11000, East = 6000, West = 2000)
  • Total sales by Product (Laptop = 18000, Phone = 7000, Tablet = 2000)
  • Sales by Region AND Product (a 4x3 grid showing every combination)
  • Average sales by Region
  • Count of sales by Product
  • % of total sales by Region
PivotTables are interactive – you can rearrange fields, filter, and drill down into data. Try dragging fields to different areas to see different views!

PivotCharts – Visualize Your PivotTable

A PivotChart is a chart that's connected to a PivotTable. When you filter the PivotTable, the chart updates automatically – perfect for dashboards.

  • Select your PivotTable.
  • Go to Insert → PivotChart.
  • Choose your chart type (Column, Bar, Line, Pie, etc.).
  • The chart is interactive – use the filter buttons to change what's shown.
  • You can also create a PivotChart directly from data (Insert → PivotChart).
  • PivotCharts support all the same formatting options as regular charts.
PivotCharts are perfect for dashboards – they update automatically when your data changes. No need to recreate charts!

Slicers – Interactive Filters

Slicers are visual filters that make it easy for users to filter PivotTables and PivotCharts. They look like buttons and are much more user-friendly than dropdowns.

  • Select your PivotTable.
  • Go to Insert → Slicer.
  • Choose the fields you want to filter by (e.g., Region, Product, Year).
  • Click the buttons to filter – multiple selections are allowed (hold Ctrl).
  • Slicers can connect to multiple PivotTables (right-click → Report Connections).
  • Format slicers to match your dashboard style (Slicer tab → Slicer Styles).
  • Use Timeline slicers for date-based filtering (Insert → Timeline).
Slicers make your dashboards interactive and professional. They're one of the best features for user-friendly reports!

Build a Mini Dashboard

A dashboard is a single page that shows key metrics and visuals. For a sales dashboard, you might include:

  • KPI cards: Total Sales, Average Sale, Number of Orders, Conversion Rate – use large, bold numbers.
  • Chart 1: Sales by Product (column chart) – shows which products are performing best.
  • Chart 2: Monthly Trend (line chart) – shows growth or decline over time.
  • Chart 3: Sales by Region (pie chart) – shows geographic distribution.
  • PivotTable: Summary table with key metrics – detailed breakdown.
  • Filters: Allow users to filter by date, region, or product using slicers.
  • Conditional Formatting: Highlight top performers or flag issues.
  • Sparklines: Mini charts showing trends for individual rows.
A good dashboard tells a story at a glance. Keep it clean, consistent, and focused on the key metrics that matter.

Dashboard Design Tips

  • Keep it simple: Don't overcrowd – show only what matters. Less is often more.
  • Use consistent colors: Use a color palette and stick to it. Use brand colors if possible.
  • Label everything: Clear titles, axis labels, and data labels – users should understand everything.
  • Use white space: Give elements room to breathe – don't cram everything together.
  • Make it interactive: Add slicers or filters so users can explore the data.
  • Test with real users: Make sure it's easy to understand and use.
  • Update regularly: Keep your dashboard refreshed with the latest data.
  • Use a grid layout: Align elements for a clean, professional look.
  • Consider your audience: What do they need to see? What decisions will they make?
Good design makes your data easy to understand and act on. A well-designed dashboard is a powerful decision-making tool.

Sparklines – Mini Charts Inside Cells

Sparklines are mini charts that fit inside a single cell – perfect for showing trends at a glance without taking up space.

  • Line Sparkline: Shows trends over time – great for sales data.
  • Column Sparkline: Shows individual values – good for comparing categories.
  • Win/Loss Sparkline: Shows positive/negative values – great for binary data.
  • How to create: Select a cell → Insert → Sparklines → Choose type → Select data range.
  • Customize: Use the Sparkline Design tab to change color, markers, and style.
Sparklines are perfect for dashboards – they show trends without using up valuable chart space.

Macros – Automate Repetitive Tasks (Introduction)

Macros record your actions so you can replay them with one click. They're great for automating repetitive tasks.

  • Record a Macro: Developer → Record Macro → Perform actions → Stop Recording.
  • Run a Macro: Developer → Macros → Select → Run.
  • Assign to a button: Insert a shape → Right-click → Assign Macro.
  • Save macros: Save as .xlsm (macro-enabled workbook).
  • Enable macros: You'll need to enable macros when opening a .xlsm file.
  • VBA: The programming language behind macros – for more advanced automation.
Macros can save hours of repetitive work. Start with simple tasks like formatting or data cleaning.

Security – Protect Your Work

  • Protect Sheet: Review → Protect Sheet – prevent users from editing specific cells.
  • Protect Workbook: Review → Protect Workbook – prevent users from adding, moving, or deleting sheets.
  • Encrypt with Password: File → Info → Protect Workbook → Encrypt with Password.
  • Lock Cells: Select cells → Format Cells → Protection → Locked → Protect Sheet.
  • Hide Formulas: Format Cells → Protection → Hidden – formulas won't show in the formula bar.
  • Mark as Final: File → Info → Protect Workbook → Mark as Final – makes it read-only.
  • Digital Signatures: Add a digital signature to verify authenticity.
Security is important when sharing sensitive data. Always use passwords and protection for confidential files.
